Inside a day

Every day has the same three moves

Theory that fits on one screen. A lab you actually run. Then a plain-English reason it matters to a security professional. Here's the real Day 3.

Opening a TCP connection

TCP is connection-oriented: before a single byte of data moves, both sides agree on starting sequence numbers and window sizes. That agreement is the three-way handshake.

  1. SYN — the client picks a random initial sequence number (ISN) and sends a segment with the SYN flag set.
  2. SYN-ACK — the server replies with its own ISN, and acknowledges the client's by sending client ISN + 1.
  3. ACK — the client acknowledges the server's ISN. Both sides move to ESTABLISHED and data can flow.
client server | SYN seq=x | | -------------------------------------> | | SYN, ACK seq=y ack=x+1 | | <------------------------------------- | | ACK seq=x+1 ack=y+1 | | -------------------------------------> | | ESTABLISHED |

UDP does none of this. It fires a datagram and forgets — which is why DNS, DHCP and VoIP use it, and why it is trivially spoofable.

Lab 3.3 — Catch a handshake in the wild

Tools: Wireshark (free). Time: 15 minutes. Target: your own machine's traffic only.

  1. Open Wireshark and start a capture on the interface carrying your internet traffic (usually Wi-Fi or eth0).
  2. Paste this into the display filter bar and press Enter — it isolates connection openings:
tcp.flags.syn == 1 && tcp.flags.ack == 0
  1. In a browser, load any site you own or a public test page. Packets appear immediately.
  2. Click a SYN packet, then Right-click → Follow → TCP Stream to isolate that conversation.
  3. Clear the filter and re-apply tcp.stream eq 0 to see all three packets in order.

Expected output — the first three rows of your conversation:

No. Source Destination Proto Info 1 192.168.1.24 93.184.216.34 TCP [SYN] Seq=0 Win=64240 2 93.184.216.34 192.168.1.24 TCP [SYN, ACK] Seq=0 Ack=1 3 192.168.1.24 93.184.216.34 TCP [ACK] Seq=1 Ack=1

Wireshark shows relative sequence numbers by default. Turn that off in Preferences → Protocols → TCP to see the real ISNs.

Why this matters for cybersecurity

Almost every scan, every flood, and every hijack is a lie told about the handshake.

Attack side. A SYN scan sends step 1 and never sends step 3 — fast, and historically quiet in logs. A SYN flood sends millions of step 1s to exhaust the server's half-open connection table. Session hijacking means guessing sequence numbers well enough to inject a segment the server accepts.

Defence side. Once you can read a handshake, half-open connections in netstat, SYN-heavy IDS alerts, and RST storms stop being noise and start being evidence. Detection rules for scanning are written directly against these flags.

Questions

Before you start

Do I need any networking background?

None. Day 1 starts at "what is an IP address". If you can open a terminal and follow instructions, you can finish the course. Some comfort with the command line helps, but every command is given in full for Windows, macOS and Linux.

Does this really cost nothing?

Yes. Wireshark and GNS3 are open source. Cisco Packet Tracer is free through Cisco's Networking Academy. Everything else — ping, dig, ss, your browser's DevTools — is already on your computer. No lab environment to rent, no VPS, no hardware.

Is any of this illegal to practise?

Nothing in the course is, because every lab targets either your own machine, your own home network, a simulator running on your laptop, or a public sample capture. Scanning, sniffing or spoofing on networks you do not own or have written permission to test is a crime in most jurisdictions. Day 5 says so in a box you cannot miss, and the attack labs are deliberately built inside Packet Tracer for exactly this reason.

How long does each day take?

Ninety minutes to two hours, split roughly 40% reading and 60% doing. Day 7 is longer — budget around two and a half hours for the capstone. Nothing expires, so a "day" can be a week if that's your pace.

Where is my progress stored?

In your browser's local storage, on your device only. There is no account, no server and no analytics. Clearing site data or using a different browser resets it — and there's a reset button in the sidebar if you want to start over deliberately.

What should I do after Day 7?

The course ends with a "where to go next" section: free rooms on TryHackMe and Hack The Box, the specific RFCs worth reading, guidance on whether a certification is worth your time, and the capture repositories to keep practising against.
